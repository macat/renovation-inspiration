# Budapest Renovation Inspiration
## Wood · Stone · Natural Materials — From Bauhaus to Today

*A curated collection of 30 houses spanning 1922–2023 — organized in three chapters. From the classic mid-century houses of Wright and Aalto, through contemporary architects carrying forward the same DNA, to Bauhaus buildings warmed with natural materials. All chosen for a Budapest house renovation that honors its Bauhaus heritage while bringing in mid-century warmth, craft, and nature.*

**The north star:** Fallingwater. Cantilevered concrete over a waterfall, anchored by native sandstone walls and walnut interiors. It's not about copying Wright — it's about understanding why stone, wood, and water feel like they belong together.

**The context:** A ~300 sqm house in Budapest with Bauhaus elements. The renovation needs to honor that geometric heritage while layering in the warmth of natural wood and stone. Clean lines meet organic materials.

# I. Classic Mid-Century (1922–1975)
*The canon. These are the houses that defined organic modernism — where stone, wood, and glass come together to dissolve the boundary between architecture and nature.*

---

## Frank Lloyd Wright

### 1. Fallingwater
**Edgar J. Kaufmann Sr. Residence** · Mill Run, Pennsylvania · 1939

- **Architect:** Frank Lloyd Wright
- **Materials:** Native sandstone (quarried on-site), reinforced concrete, steel, black walnut, glass
- **Why it matters:** The definitive example of organic architecture — a house that grows out of its landscape. The stone walls are built from the same sandstone ledge the house sits on. Walnut millwork throughout. The cantilevered terraces hover over Bear Run creek. Every material is either local or serves to blur the line between inside and outside.
- **Renovation takeaway:** Stone that comes from the site. Wood that gets warmer with age. Wright's integration of built-in furniture (stone and walnut) is directly applicable to a Budapest renovation. The idea that a house doesn't sit *on* the land — it's *part* of the land.
- **Visit:** [fallingwater.org](https://fallingwater.org)

### 2. Kentuck Knob
**I.N. Hagan House** · Chalk Hill, Pennsylvania · 1956

- **Architect:** Frank Lloyd Wright
- **Materials:** Tidewater red cypress, native sandstone, copper roof
- **Why it matters:** Built 7 miles from Fallingwater, but completely different. A single-story Usonian hugging a hilltop. The cypress has aged to silver-grey. The hexagonal grid plan creates flowing spaces without corridors.
- **Renovation takeaway:** More achievable than Fallingwater. Material continuity — the same stone flows from exterior walls to interior fireplace. Hexagonal modules create interesting angles without chaos.
- **Visit:** [kentuckknob.com](https://kentuckknob.com)

### 3. Taliesin West
**Frank Lloyd Wright's Winter Home & Studio** · Scottsdale, Arizona · 1937

- **Architect:** Frank Lloyd Wright
- **Materials:** Desert rubblestone (volcanic rock set in concrete), redwood, canvas/glass, steel
- **Why it matters:** Massive desert masonry walls — local volcanic rocks set into concrete forms, creating a textured surface that looks like the desert floor stood up. Redwood beams soften the interior.
- **Renovation takeaway:** Rubblestone walls create texture and depth you can't get from smooth-cut stone. Rough stone + finished wood is one of the most powerful material pairings. Massive thermal mass indoors is excellent for Budapest's continental climate.
- **Visit:** [franklloydwright.org](https://franklloydwright.org)

### 4. Seth Peterson Cottage
**Lake Delton, Wisconsin** · 1958

- **Architect:** Frank Lloyd Wright
- **Materials:** Sandstone, wood, glass
- **Why it matters:** Wright's smallest commission — 880 sq ft. A massive sandstone fireplace as the anchor, warm wood ceilings, floor-to-ceiling glass. Wright called it his "best small building."
- **Renovation takeaway:** One bold material gesture (the stone fireplace wall) gives the whole space an identity. At ~80 sqm, directly relevant to individual rooms in a 300 sqm house.
- **Stay:** Rentable overnight at [sethpeterson.org](https://sethpeterson.org)

## Richard Neutra

### 5. Kaufmann Desert House
**Palm Springs, California** · 1946

- **Architect:** Richard Neutra
- **Materials:** Utah buff sandstone, steel, glass, aluminum
- **Why it matters:** Built for the same Kaufmann who commissioned Fallingwater. Pinwheel plan, four wings around a central pool. Utah sandstone anchors the house to the desert while steel and glass dissolve boundaries. Neutra optimized every compass point for shade and breeze.
- **Renovation takeaway:** Stone as anchor — one material giving weight and permanence, everything else light and transparent. A natural stone wall serving as both exterior and interior finish, running continuously through the glass line.

## Alvar Aalto

### 6. Villa Mairea
**Noormarkku, Finland** · 1939

- **Architect:** Alvar Aalto (with Aino Aalto)
- **Materials:** Pine, birch, teak, brick, tile, natural stone, render, steel
- **Why it matters:** Every room uses different materials and textures. Exterior walls shift from render to wood slats to brick. Inside, birch columns wrapped with rattan evoke a forest. The sauna uses rough stone. The swimming pool is shaped like a natural pond. The richest material palette on this list.
- **Renovation takeaway:** You don't need material uniformity — you need material *intention*. Each space gets its own treatment. This is how to renovate room by room without it feeling disjointed. Finnish wood culture at its highest — directly relevant to Central European sensibility.
- **Visit:** [villamairea.fi](https://villamairea.fi)

### 7. Muuratsalo Experimental House
**Muuratsalo Island, Finland** · 1953

- **Architect:** Alvar Aalto
- **Materials:** Over 50 types of brick and tile, painted wood, copper, stone
- **Why it matters:** Aalto's summer house and material laboratory. The courtyard walls are a patchwork of different brick and tile experiments — sizes, colors, patterns, mortar techniques. A built mood board.
- **Renovation takeaway:** The idea of a material "test wall." Also: a courtyard plan (rooms around outdoor space) oriented to trap low northern sun — same principle works in Budapest.

## Marcel Breuer 🇭🇺
*Born in Pécs, 1902. Studied at the Bauhaus. Designed the Wassily Chair at 23. Emigrated to the US, where he built some of the finest stone-and-wood houses in New England.*

### 8. Breuer House II
**New Canaan, Connecticut** · 1948

- **Architect:** Marcel Breuer
- **Materials:** Fieldstone, cypress wood, glass, steel, poured concrete
- **Why it matters:** Breuer built this as his own family home. Cantilevered over a slope, with a fieldstone base and wood-framed upper level. A Hungarian modernist's personal statement.
- **Renovation takeaway:** Deeply relevant — a Hungarian architect's house. Fieldstone base + wood upper is directly applicable: heavy stone on the ground, warm wood above. Bold primary colors against natural materials.

### 9. Stillman House I
**Litchfield, Connecticut** · 1951

- **Architect:** Marcel Breuer
- **Materials:** Fieldstone, wood, glass, steel, concrete
- **Why it matters:** Local fieldstone walls — the same stone used in colonial Connecticut walls — ground the modernist glass-and-steel structure. Interior features warm wood ceilings and a fireplace mural by Alexander Calder.
- **Renovation takeaway:** Use local stone. For Budapest: Hungarian limestone, Buda hills dolomite, or Danube river stone. Let the stone tell people where the house is.

### 10. Gagarin House I
**Litchfield, Connecticut** · 1957

- **Architect:** Marcel Breuer
- **Materials:** Fieldstone, bluestone terraces, wood, glass, concrete
- **Why it matters:** The monumental fieldstone chimney. Bluestone terraces connecting house to hillside. A tree trunk embedded in the concrete fireplace surround — nature built into the structure.
- **Renovation takeaway:** The tree-trunk-in-concrete detail. Leaving evidence of the landscape in the architecture. Bluestone terraces — elegant, durable, aging beautifully.

## Eero & Eliel Saarinen

### 11. Miller House
**Columbus, Indiana** · 1957

- **Architect:** Eero Saarinen (interiors: Alexander Girard, landscape: Dan Kiley)
- **Materials:** Marble, slate, steel, glass, walnut, leather
- **Why it matters:** Three masters collaborating. Girard's legendary interiors — a sunken "conversation pit" around a fireplace, a storage wall with 1,000+ collected objects. Kiley's landscape of honey locust trees.
- **Renovation takeaway:** The conversation pit — a warm, lowered center where people gather around fire. Girard's collections approach (open shelving with curated objects) is perfect for a home valuing character over minimalism.

### 12. Saarinen House (Cranbrook)
**Bloomfield Hills, Michigan** · 1930

- **Architect:** Eliel Saarinen (with Loja Saarinen)
- **Materials:** Brick, copper, wood paneling, custom textiles, wrought iron
- **Why it matters:** Gesamtkunstwerk — the Finnish architect and his wife designed everything: building, furniture, rugs, fixtures, curtains. Wood paneling, handwoven textiles, custom metalwork. Arts & Crafts warmth meets Nordic modernism.
- **Renovation takeaway:** Renovation means designing *everything* in a room. Material continuity (same wood in paneling, furniture, trim) creates coherence. Finnish/Nordic sensibility is culturally close to Hungarian design values.
- **Visit:** [cranbrook.edu](https://www.cranbrook.edu)

## John Lautner

### 13. Sheats–Goldstein Residence
**Los Angeles, California** · 1963

- **Architect:** John Lautner
- **Materials:** Poured-in-place concrete, natural boulders (integrated), glass, steel
- **Why it matters:** Built on a sandstone ledge that becomes interior — boulders protrude through floor and walls. Coffered concrete ceiling creates a cave-like canopy. Lautner pushed organic architecture to its most dramatic expression.
- **Renovation takeaway:** Don't hide the site — celebrate it. If the Budapest property has rock, old walls, or existing structure worth exposing, make it a feature.

## MLTW / Charles Moore

### 14. The Sea Ranch (Condominium One)
**Sonoma County, California** · 1965

- **Architect:** MLTW (Moore, Lyndon, Turnbull, Whitaker)
- **Materials:** Rough-sawn redwood (vertical board siding), heavy timber framing, concrete block
- **Why it matters:** Unpainted redwood weathering to silver-grey. Buildings angled to deflect Pacific wind. Lawrence Halprin's landscape plan preserved the coastal meadow. Architecture as environmental stewardship.
- **Renovation takeaway:** Naturally weathering wood exterior. For Budapest, European larch or Douglas fir weathers similarly. The cluster-of-volumes approach creates intimate spaces.

## George Nakashima

### 15. George Nakashima Compound
**New Hope, Pennsylvania** · 1946–1974

- **Architect/Builder:** George Nakashima
- **Materials:** Local stone, reinforced concrete, walnut, cherry, oak, rosewood
- **Why it matters:** 21 structures built over 28 years. Each building an experiment in wood joinery. Nakashima's philosophy: every tree has a soul, and the woodworker's job is to reveal it.
- **Renovation takeaway:** The ultimate wood reference. Don't specify "oak flooring" — go to the timber yard, pick the boards yourself, let the wood's character come through. Live edges, natural grain, honest joinery.
- **Visit:** [nakashimawoodworkers.com](https://nakashimawoodworkers.com)

## Albert Frey

### 16. Frey House II
**Palm Springs, California** · 1964

- **Architect:** Albert Frey
- **Materials:** Steel, glass, corrugated metal, desert boulder (integrated through interior)
- **Why it matters:** 800 sq ft. A massive boulder pushes through the bedroom wall and becomes the headboard. Equal parts industrial and natural.
- **Renovation takeaway:** One powerful natural element can define an entire house. Radical architecture doesn't require a big budget.

# II. Contemporary Houses with Mid-Century DNA (1994–2023)
*Modern architects who carry forward the same values — natural materials, honest structure, connection to landscape — with contemporary technology and sensibility. These show what mid-century principles look like when built today.*

---

## Glenn Murcutt

### 17. Simpson-Lee House
**Mount Wilson, New South Wales, Australia** · 1994

- **Architect:** Glenn Murcutt
- **Materials:** Steel, glass, sandstone, concrete, zinc-clad roof
- **Why it matters:** "Touches the earth lightly." Sandstone walls at the base anchor the house to its Blue Mountains ridge. Adjustable louvres respond to sun, wind, and bushfire risk. Environmentally tuned like an instrument.
- **Renovation takeaway:** Heavy sandstone base, topped by lighter materials. Murcutt's obsessive environmental performance (passive solar, natural ventilation, rainwater) is exactly what a modern renovation should achieve.

---

## Peter Zumthor

### 18. Zumthor's Own Home
**Haldenstein, Switzerland** · 2005

- **Architect:** Peter Zumthor
- **Materials:** Swiss spruce, Canadian maple (interior), glass, concrete foundation
- **Why it matters:** A simple wooden box where the wood is so precisely selected and joined that surfaces feel like furniture. Large windows frame specific Alpine views. Almost nothing decorative — beauty is entirely in material quality and proportions.
- **Renovation takeaway:** Quality over quantity. One species of wood, perfectly executed, is worth ten exotic materials poorly installed. Find the best Hungarian oak you can, and let it be the whole story.

### 19. Zumthor Vacation Houses, Leis
**Vals, Graubünden, Switzerland** · 2009

- **Architect:** Peter Zumthor (interiors: Annalisa Zumthor)
- **Materials:** Larch timber (exterior), spruce (interior), local stone, wood-fired ovens
- **Why it matters:** Three timber houses that look like they've been there for centuries — but inside, they're contemporary and meticulously crafted. Wood-fired ovens, saunas, panoramic windows.
- **Renovation takeaway:** Be excellent but don't shout. The wood-fired oven as a central element is culturally resonant in Hungary (cserépkályha tradition). New construction that respects its context.
- **Stay:** [zfrh.ch](https://www.zfrh.ch)

## Olson Kundig / Tom Kundig

### 20. The Pierre
**San Juan Island, Washington** · 2010

- **Architect:** Tom Kundig / Olson Kundig
- **Materials:** Native stone (excavated and re-used), concrete, steel, glass, green roof
- **Why it matters:** Named "The Pierre" (French for stone). Conceived as a bunker nestled into a rock outcropping. Excavated stone was crushed and mixed into concrete for the floors, so the house is literally *made of* its site. A green roof makes it disappear from above. Raw stone walls inside create a cave-like atmosphere that's somehow luxurious.
- **Renovation takeaway:** The most Fallingwater-like contemporary house on this list — the same philosophy of building *from* the site, not *on* it. Shows how mid-century organic values translate to 21st-century construction. The hand-craft detailing (Kundig's signature gizmos — massive steel pivot doors, hand-operated mechanisms) adds warmth to raw materials.

## Studio MK27 / Marcio Kogan

### 21. Catuçaba Farm
**Catuçaba, São Paulo, Brazil** · 2016

- **Architect:** Studio MK27 / Marcio Kogan (with Lair Reis)
- **Materials:** FSC-certified eucalyptus, local clay brick, timber frame, glass
- **Why it matters:** A slender timber house at 1,500m elevation, raised on pillars over the landscape. Off-grid (solar power, rainwater), built with local materials. Brazil's first Platinum-certified Green Building Council project. The eucalyptus screen creates a dappled light effect throughout.
- **Renovation takeaway:** How Brazilian modernism — descended from the same mid-century lineage — handles wood today. The screen/brise-soleil approach (slatted wood filtering light and view) is perfect for south-facing Budapest windows. Shows that sustainable + beautiful aren't contradictory.

## Norm Architects

### 22. Archipelago House
**Swedish Coast** · 2020

- **Architect:** Norm Architects (Copenhagen)
- **Materials:** Pine cladding, timber framing, stone, custom furniture (Karimoku collaboration)
- **Why it matters:** Four wooden volumes interlocked on a rocky Swedish coastline, inspired by local boathouse traditions. Blends Scandinavian minimalism with Japanese craft (Danish-Japanese furniture collaboration). Double-height living space, natural pine walls throughout, terraced deck following the sloping site.
- **Renovation takeaway:** The Nordic-Japanese crossover is directly relevant — both cultures share Central Europe's reverence for wood, craft, and restraint. Shows how to use one wood species (pine) as both structure and finish. The multi-volume approach (connected pavilions) works well in renovation when adding to an existing house.

## Kengo Kuma

### 23. Suteki House
**Portland, Oregon** · 2023

- **Architect:** Kengo Kuma & Associates
- **Materials:** Local cedar, stone, glass, timber
- **Why it matters:** Kuma explicitly cited Fallingwater as inspiration. The L-shaped house wraps around a giant oak tree and a stream, using "borrowed scenery" (shakkei) — a 17th-century Japanese landscaping principle. Cedar cladding and stone base connect to the Pacific Northwest landscape. Seamless indoor-outdoor spaces.
- **Renovation takeaway:** A direct contemporary descendant of Wright's thinking, filtered through Japanese sensibility. The "borrowed scenery" concept — framing specific trees or views from specific rooms — is powerful for a Budapest house where you can frame garden views or distant hills.

### 25. Haus Balma
**Vals, Graubünden, Switzerland** · 2022

- **Architect:** Kengo Kuma & Associates
- **Materials:** Vals quartzite stone (882 panels), local timber, glass
- **Why it matters:** In the same village as Zumthor's Therme Vals. Kuma created a building wrapped in 882 stone panels that appear to float — a "curtain of stone." The interior blends timber framing with stone surfaces, referencing both Japanese pagodas and Alpine chalets. Traditional Valser craftsmanship meets contemporary detailing.
- **Renovation takeaway:** Stone as screen, not just mass. The floating stone panels suggest you can use stone in surprising, lightweight ways — not just massive walls but thin veneers, suspended screens, or perforated panels. This is how stone can work in a Bauhaus geometry house.

## John Pawson

### 24. Home Farm
**Cotswolds, England** · 2019

- **Architect:** John Pawson
- **Materials:** Local Cotswold stone, lime plaster, Douglas fir, concrete, elm
- **Why it matters:** A 1610 farmhouse renovated by the world's most rigorous minimalist. Pawson kept the ancient stone walls and added a 45-meter-long contemporary wing. The interiors are severe but warm — local stone floors, lime-plastered walls, Douglas fir windows, and elm furniture (Donald Judd chairs). Three kitchens. Zero clutter.
- **Renovation takeaway:** The masterclass in renovating a historic building with a minimalist hand. Pawson proves you don't need decoration when your materials are right. Keep the old stone walls, add new elements in a single warm wood. This is the exact Bauhaus-meets-organic-warmth sensibility the Budapest house needs.

## Hungarian & Central European

### 26. Farkasrét Mortuary Chapel
**Budapest XII. district** · 1977

- **Architect:** Imre Makovecz
- **Materials:** Timber (ribbed structure), wood, stone, plaster
- **Why it matters:** Not a house, but the most important reference for Hungarian organic architecture. The interior is a massive ribbed timber structure evoking a human rib cage. Makovecz (1935–2011), influenced by Wright and Steiner, built with wood and stone in a distinctly Hungarian idiom for his entire career.
- **Renovation takeaway:** The Hungarian equivalent of Wright. Proof that modernism needn't abandon craft, nature, or national identity. There is a *Hungarian* tradition of working with wood and stone that doesn't need importing from Scandinavia.
- **Visit:** Farkasréti Cemetery, Németvölgyi út, Budapest XII.

### 27. Wooden House, Algyő
**Algyő, Hungary** · 2016

- **Architects:** András Varsányi, Péter Pozsár, Norbert Vas
- **Materials:** Timber frame, wood cladding, stone, glass
- **Why it matters:** A contemporary Hungarian farmhouse reinterpreting the traditional tanya (farmstead). Central courtyard, wood-clad exterior, timber framing — rural traditions with a modern interior.
- **Renovation takeaway:** The most directly relevant example — contemporary Hungarian architects using local materials and traditions. The courtyard plan is deeply rooted in Hungarian residential architecture.

# III. Bauhaus + Natural Materials
*Budapest has one of Europe's strongest Bauhaus traditions — over 300 modernist buildings from 1930–1948. The challenge: how do you honor that geometric, functional heritage while bringing in the warmth of natural wood and stone? These examples show the way.*

---

### 28. Napraforgó Street Houses
**Budapest II. district (Pasarét)** · 1931

- **Architects:** 22 houses by 28 different architects
- **Materials:** Concrete, plaster, glass, steel — the original Bauhaus palette
- **Why it matters:** Budapest's own Weissenhof Estate. In 1931, the city commissioned 22 detached houses on a single street, each by a different architect group, all in the Bauhaus/International Style. Flat roofs, ribbon windows, geometric volumes, white plaster walls. The street survives largely intact — a living museum of Hungarian modernism. This is the architectural DNA your house carries.
- **Renovation takeaway:** Study these houses for the *geometry* — the proportions, the window rhythms, the flat roof details. Then imagine them with the warmth of Villa Mairea's interiors: keep the clean lines and geometric volumes, but swap white plaster for textured stone, add wood paneling inside, replace steel windows with warm timber frames. That's the recipe.
- **Visit:** Napraforgó utca, Budapest II. Walk the whole street.

### 29. Walter Rózsi Villa
**Budapest XI. district** · 1936

- **Architect:** József Fischer
- **Materials:** Concrete, plaster, glass, terrazzo, wood
- **Why it matters:** One of Budapest's finest modernist houses, now housing the Hungarian Museum of Architecture's first permanent exhibition on modern residential houses. Fischer designed crisp white volumes with grid-patterned glass walls and a stepped mass system. Restored after decades of neglect.
- **Renovation takeaway:** This is what Budapest Bauhaus looks like at its best — the white cube tradition. The museum is worth visiting specifically to understand the baseline you're working with before adding natural materials. Note how even in the original, wood floors and terrazzo brought warmth to the white walls.
- **Visit:** Hungarian Museum of Architecture (KÉK), Budapest

### 30. Sarolta Huttl — Budapest Bauhaus Apartment Renovation
**Budapest** · 2016

- **Designer:** Sarolta Huttl
- **Materials:** Oak flooring, concrete tiles, Valchromat panels, teal cabinetry, white tile, original 1930s plaster details
- **Why it matters:** This is the most directly relevant example. Huttl took a 1930s Bauhaus villa apartment in Budapest and renovated it with contemporary materials while preserving the original structure. She kept the geometric volumes and high ceilings, restored original plaster details, then layered in warm oak flooring and bold color accents. Natural light flows through the original large windows.
- **Renovation takeaway:** The closest precedent to what you're planning. Huttl shows how to keep Bauhaus proportions intact while warming the interior with oak and natural materials. Her color strategy — teal and white against warm wood — is a lesson in making Bauhaus spaces feel inhabited, not clinical.

### 31. B-Architecten — 1930s Belgian House Renovation
**Belgium** · ~2020

- **Architect:** B-Architecten
- **Materials:** Travertine flooring, bespoke furnishings, original steel frames, curved staircase, skylit bathroom
- **Why it matters:** A 1930s modernist house renovated by blending its original details (curved staircase, steel window frames) with contemporary luxury materials. Travertine floors replaced the original flooring, adding warmth and texture while respecting the geometric language. A skylit bathroom brings light into the core. Mid-century furniture complements the Bauhaus architecture.
- **Renovation takeaway:** Travertine is the bridge material — it's stone, so it's natural and warm, but it's flat and precise, so it respects Bauhaus geometry. Hungarian travertine from the Bükk Mountains would be historically and materially perfect for the Budapest house. This project shows how travertine flooring unifies rooms that were designed in the white-and-terrazzo Bauhaus tradition.

### 32. Villa Rezek
**Vienna** · 1933 (restored 2022)

- **Architect:** Hans Glas (restoration: Maximilian Eisenköck)
- **Materials:** Original 1930s structure, restored interiors, period-appropriate materials
- **Why it matters:** A Central European Bauhaus contemporary — built just 200 km from Budapest, in the same cultural milieu. The 2022 restoration preserved the original design intent: clean geometric volumes, large windows, functional layout. Now a temporary museum showing how 1930s Viennese modernism lived.
- **Renovation takeaway:** The closest cultural analog to a Budapest Bauhaus house. Vienna and Budapest shared architects, ideas, and material suppliers in the 1930s. Study the restored details — window profiles, floor materials, wall treatments — for period-appropriate inspiration that can then be warmed with contemporary wood and stone additions.

# Design Principles Distilled

1. **Local materials first.** Wright used Pennsylvania sandstone. Breuer used Connecticut fieldstone. Zumthor used Swiss larch. The best material is the one already in the landscape.

2. **One hero per space.** A stone fireplace wall, a wood-paneled ceiling, a concrete floor. Each room gets one material "hero" — everything else supports it.

3. **Let materials age.** Redwood turns silver. Oak darkens. Copper goes green. Design for it, don't fight it.

4. **Built-in furniture.** Wright, Breuer, Aalto — all designed furniture as architecture. Window seats, shelves, desks, and benches save space and create coherence.

5. **Blur inside and outside.** A stone wall from garden to living room. A floor continuing onto a terrace. A window framing a specific tree.

6. **Honest structure.** Exposed stone, visible timber beams, raw concrete — the structure *is* the decoration.

7. **Light is a material.** Ando's slits, Wright's clerestories, Aalto's skylights. Control, direct, and celebrate natural light.

8. **Warmth over coolness.** Wood, stone, textiles, fire. This is organic modernism, not white-box minimalism.

9. **Respect the Bauhaus bones.** For a house with Bauhaus heritage: keep the geometry, keep the proportions, keep the clean lines. Add warmth through material, not ornament.

# How to Use This Collection

1. **Pick your stone.** Visit a Hungarian quarry. Hungarian travertine bridges Bauhaus precision and mid-century warmth.
2. **Pick your wood.** One primary species (Hungarian oak) and one accent (walnut for contrast).
3. **Identify 3-5 houses** from each section that resonate. Print specific details — a fireplace, a window, a ceiling.
